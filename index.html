About

Networks, security, and the hardware in between.

I am a Ph.D. student in Computer Science at Oklahoma State University, advised by Dr. H. B. Acharya, with an expected graduation in May 2028. I began my doctoral work at the Rochester Institute of Technology in 2021 and moved to OSU to continue with the same advisor.

My research sits where programmable data planes meet network security. I write P4 for Intel Tofino switches to implement deep packet inspection, stateful filtering, and intrusion-detection functions at line rate, and I study the limits (stages, memory, parsing, instruction set) that decide what can and cannot be done in the switch. I also build Internet measurement tools that tell hard failures from soft ones and locate where interference happens, which I used to study Internet access during the Russia–Ukraine war.

Outside the lab I teach (adjunct instructor for cybersecurity and software-development courses at RIT, teaching assistant at OSU), mentor student researchers, review artifacts and papers for systems and networking venues, and serve as President of OSU's Computer Science Graduate Student Association.

Research

Three threads, one question: how do you make a fast, constrained network device trustworthy?

Programmable data-plane security on Intel Tofino

2021 – present · RIT, then Oklahoma State

Can a switch that only does simple arithmetic, has a few stages and a few MB of fast memory, still act as an application-layer firewall or intrusion-detection system? I implement DPI and pattern matching, stateful filtering, and detection logic in P4 on Tofino and Tofino2 and measure what the hardware actually allows.

  • Packet-analysis and filtering algorithms built on recirculation, cross-packet flow state, and precomputed lookups.
  • Prototyped and evaluated on Tofino hardware with the P4 Studio SDE and a BF-RT control plane.
  • Systematized the field in Programmable Data Planes for Network Security (NeTCoM 2025).

Internet censorship & resilience measurement

2022 – present

When a network is under political or military stress, which sites stop working, where along the path does it happen, and is it damage or deliberate filtering? I build measurement tools that answer those questions and used them to study Internet access during the first months of the Russia–Ukraine war.

  • A classifier for soft failures (pages that load but are not what was asked for), beyond hard connection failures.
  • A multi-protocol traceroute implementation that localizes where interference occurs on a path.
  • Longitudinal measurements from Ukraine, Russia, and the U.S., March–July 2022 (ACM ASIA CCS 2023).

Intent-based networking for programmable hardware

2026 – present · Keysight Technologies · R&D Software Engineering Intern

Can an operator state what the network should do in plain language and a protocol specification, and get a correct hardware implementation out? As a part-time, remote R&D Software Engineering Intern (FPGA development for AI infrastructure testing solutions) I work on the software side of a pipeline that turns intent and specifications into C/C++ implementations targeted for FPGAs.

  • Designed the intent-to-implementation architecture: intent and specification ingestion, a C/C++ intermediate path, and a proposed direct intent-to-HDL path.
  • Collaborating with FPGA-focused interns on the hardware side.

Directions I am pursuing

Open questions behind my dissertation work. These are directions, not finished results.

  1. Correct, not just fast. When flow state, recirculation, and enforcement timing compete for the same pipeline, how do we guarantee that inspection actually completes for every packet we forward?
  2. Inspection capacity. Forwarded throughput is not the same as correctly inspected throughput. What are the right ways to measure when a security function exhausts a switch's stages, memory, or recirculation bandwidth?
  3. Heterogeneous data paths. How should security state move between ASIC switches, SmartNICs/DPUs, FPGAs, and hosts without losing exactly-once effects or safety during updates?
  4. Honest measurement. How much do measurement probes themselves bias what we conclude about censorship and resilience, and how do we correct for it?

  1. NeTCoM 2025 Conference paper · systematization First author

    Programmable Data Planes for Network Security

    Gursimran Singh, H. B. Acharya, and Minseok Kwon

    17th International Conference on Networks & Communications (NeTCoM 2025), Computer Science & Information Technology (CS & IT) vol. 15, no. 13, pp. 163–180, July 2025.

    Surveys how P4 programmable switches have been used for attack mitigation (spoofing, DDoS), firewalls and middleboxes (including DPI), in-network cryptography, and in-switch machine learning. The emphasis is on the non-obvious design techniques, such as recirculate-and-truncate and lookup-table precomputation, that make these functions feasible under tight memory and instruction-set limits, and on what remains open.

    DOI arXiv PDF Scholar

    The emergence of programmable data planes, and particularly switches supporting the P4 language, has transformed network security by enabling customized, line-rate packet processing. These switches, originally intended for flexible forwarding, now play a broader role: detecting and mitigating attacks such as DDoS and spoofing, enforcing next-generation firewall policies, and even supporting in-network cryptography and machine learning. These capabilities are made possible by techniques such as recirculate-and-truncate and lookup-table precomputation, which work around architectural constraints like limited memory and restricted instruction sets. In this paper, we systematize recent advances in security applications built on programmable switches, with an emphasis on the capabilities, challenges, and architectural workarounds. We highlight the non-obvious design techniques that make complex in-network security functions feasible despite the constraints of the hardware platform, and also comment on remaining issues and emerging research directions.

  2. ACM ASIA CCS 2023 Poster paper First author

    POSTER: A Cyberspace Study of the Russia–Ukraine War

    Gursimran Singh and H. B. Acharya

    Proceedings of the 2023 ACM Asia Conference on Computer and Communications Security (ASIA CCS '23), Melbourne, pp. 1016–1018, July 2023.

    Asks whether the Internet routes around abuse: did Russia, as a major Internet power, use its network to deny access to Ukraine during the first months of the war? We measured hard and soft failures of website access from Ukraine, Russia, and the United States between March and July 2022, built a soft-failure classifier and a multi-protocol traceroute to locate interference, and found a substantial change in access to sites from Ukraine over the period while Russian ASes were not causing significant collateral damage through filtering.

    DOI ACM DL PDF (OSU repository) Scholar

    This paper aims to investigate the resilience of the internet in the face of censorship through a current case study: the war between Russia and Ukraine. We focus on whether Russia, as a major Internet power, has been using its network to deny access to Ukraine (and whether the Internet is resilient enough to route around such abuse). We consider how Internet accessibility changed over the course of the first few months, considering both hard and soft failures of website access. Our result, in brief, is that there is a substantial difference in network access to sites from Ukraine between March and July 2022, but Russian ASes are not causing significant collateral damage by filtering. In addition, we present the tools and resources developed in the project, including a classifier to detect soft-failures and a new multi-protocol implementation of Traceroute to locate internet censorship.
